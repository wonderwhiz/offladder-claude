# Negotiate a job offer

Pay conversations go best with a researched range, one clear request and a calm tone. The goal is a fair offer they can accept without regret, not winning.

## Research the number first

1. **Start with official pay data** for the occupation and area: the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook or CareerOneStop in the US, the ONS Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings in the UK, or the national statistics office elsewhere.
2. **Add live evidence**: pay ranges in current job posts for the same title, level and city, and what people in the role say.
3. **Set a range** whose bottom is a figure they would genuinely accept.

## "What are your salary expectations?"

Give the range, tied to the role: "Based on similar roles here, I'm looking for X to Y, depending on the full package." If it's asked very early, it's fine to ask what the budgeted range is first.

## Negotiating an offer

1. Thank them and ask for a day or two to review it in writing.
2. Make one clear request backed by research: "Based on similar roles and my experience with X, I was hoping for Y. Is there flexibility?"
3. If base pay is fixed, ask about one other thing that matters: start date, title, flexible days, training budget, sign-on payment or an early review.
4. Get the final offer in writing before resigning anywhere.

Draft the actual message or talking points for them, short and polite.

## Comparing two offers

Put them side by side in one table: total pay, the daily work, the manager, growth, flexibility, commute and stability. Weight each row by how much it matters to them, score it, and then check whether the winner matches their gut. If it doesn't, ask which row they'd really weight higher.

## Counter-offers

Accepting a counter-offer makes sense only if pay was the main reason for leaving and the counter fixes it properly, in writing. If they started looking because of the work, the manager or progression, more money rarely fixes it.

## Rules

- Never invent salary figures. Point to the sources above and say when a number is an estimate.
- Don't encourage bluffing about competing offers.
- Employment law and tax questions go to an official source or an adviser.
